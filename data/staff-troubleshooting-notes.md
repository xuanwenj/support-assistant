# Staff Troubleshooting Notes

Internal notes compiled from recurring situations at the service desk. These aren't official policy — they're the kind of institutional knowledge that usually just lives in senior staff's heads. Written up here so newer staff (and this Q&A tool) can reference them.

---

## When a customer says a mixer "just stopped working"

Nine times out of ten this isn't the mixer at all — it's upstream pressure. Before anything else, ask whether anything changed recently: a new pressure-limiting valve installed, mains work done on the street, or a header tank that's run low. If the customer describes a sudden total loss of flow rather than a gradual drop, that points to a blocked aerator or a shut isolation valve more than a broken mixer body. Only escalate to a warranty claim once pressure and blockage have both been ruled out — otherwise we end up processing warranty replacements for units that were never actually faulty, which shows up badly in our return rate numbers at the end of the quarter.

## The in-wall cistern access panel question

We get this a lot: a customer with a wall-faced toilet suite says the cistern won't flush properly and asks how to open it up themselves. The access panel is usually behind the flush plate on the wall, but because the frame is installed before the wall lining goes on, the exact panel location varies by how the installer set it up — there's no single standard answer we can give over the phone. This is one of the rare cases where we should firmly redirect to a licensed plumber rather than attempt phone troubleshooting, because DIY access attempts on these units have previously caused customers to damage the wall lining trying to locate the panel.

## Bundle returns are messier than they look

Whenever a bundle purchase comes back for a partial return, resist the urge to just refund the difference and move on. The underlying per-item discount only existed because both items were bought together — pull apart a twin shower bundle and keep only the handheld unit, and technically the diverter valve that was included was priced below its normal retail rate specifically because of the bundle. If we let the customer keep the diverter at the bundle-adjusted price while returning everything else, we're effectively giving away margin on a sale that no longer qualifies for the discount that justified it. Always recalculate against individual retail pricing before finalizing a partial bundle return, not the bundle price.

## Trade account customers buying for personal use

Occasionally a trade account holder will ask for the trade price on something clearly for their own home renovation rather than a client job. We don't police this closely and it's not something staff should interrogate customers about, but it's worth knowing this exists as a known gray area — trade pricing terms don't formally distinguish between "for a client" and "for yourself" as long as the purchase goes through an active trade account.

## Old houses and pressure mismatches

A recurring pattern: customers renovating older villas assume any shower head or mixer will work with their gravity-fed system because "it's just water pressure." This isn't true, and it's worth actively bringing up even when not asked, because the failure mode isn't a dramatic burst pipe — it's just a shower that barely dribbles, and the customer comes back weeks later frustrated, well past a comfortable return window, having assumed the product was simply low quality. Catching this at the point of sale saves everyone the argument later.

## When two documents seem to disagree

If a product's stated pressure rating in the catalog doesn't match what's printed on the manufacturer's box or an older supplier bulletin, the catalog is the source of truth for customer-facing answers — it reflects our most recent supplier update. Older bulletins sometimes float around in email chains or get reprinted in outdated training material; if a number looks off compared to what's in the current catalog, assume the catalog is right and flag the discrepancy to a manager rather than quoting the older figure.
